What crypto data to give your accountant: an 11-step checklist

Your accountant decides how your crypto is taxed. Your job is to give them complete, clean data. This is the list of what to collect, and where to find it, so the work (and the bill) stays small.

Not tax advice. Rules differ a lot between countries and change often. This page only covers collecting the data; how it is taxed is a question for a qualified professional where you live.

The checklist

  1. List every place you held crypto

    Exchanges (including ones you closed or stopped using), hardware and software wallets, DeFi protocols, payment apps that sell crypto. Missing one account is the most common reason a report does not add up. Check old emails for sign-up confirmations if you are not sure.

  2. Export the full history from each exchange

    Most exchanges have a transaction or tax export (CSV) in their settings or reports section. Export all years, not just the last one: the cost of what you sell today depends on when and at what price you bought it. Ask for trades, deposits, withdrawals, conversions, rewards and fees.

  3. Export on-chain history for self-custody wallets

    For wallets you control, the block explorer of each chain (for example Etherscan, Basescan, Solscan) can export a wallet's transactions as CSV. List each address and which chain it is on. Never share seed phrases or private keys with anyone, including your accountant: an address is all they need.

  4. Collect year-end balances

    Many countries ask for what you held on 31 December (or your tax year end), sometimes with its value. Take a dated screenshot or statement of each account's balances on that day, in coins and in your currency.

  5. Record the value at each event

    Tax is calculated in your own currency. For every buy, sell, swap or income event you need the value at that moment. Exchanges usually include it; for on-chain activity, note the source you used for prices (for example CoinGecko daily close) and use the same one for everything.

  6. Separate transfers between your own wallets

    Moving coins from an exchange to your own wallet is not a sale. Mark these transfers clearly, matching the withdrawal on one side with the deposit on the other, or they may be counted as disposals or as income.

  7. Tag staking, airdrops, mining and other income

    Rewards you receive are often treated differently from trading gains, and sometimes taxed when received. Keep them on their own list with date, amount and value.

  8. Note fees

    Trading, network (gas) and withdrawal fees can change the result. Keep them in the export rather than deleting the columns.

  9. Keep DeFi and NFT activity apart

    Liquidity pools, lending, bridges, wrapped tokens and NFTs are where automatic tools make the most mistakes. Put these transactions in a separate file with a short note on what each one was, so your accountant can decide how to treat them.

  10. Check that the files add up

    For each coin: what you bought or received, minus what you sold, sent or spent, should equal your year-end balance. If it does not, something is missing, usually an account or an internal transfer.

  11. Hand everything over in one tidy folder

    One folder per year, one file per account, a list of addresses, the year-end balances, and a one-page summary of anything unusual. A tidy folder saves your accountant hours, and saves you their hourly rate.

The short version

Every account, every year, every transaction, valued in your currency, with internal transfers marked and income kept apart. If the totals match your year-end balances, your data is ready.

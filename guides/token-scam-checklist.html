How to check if a crypto token is a scam: a 12-point checklist

Most scam tokens fail the same few checks. You can run all twelve with a block explorer and a couple of free scanners, in about 15 minutes, before you put a cent in.

This is a checklist for spotting technical red flags. It is not investment advice: a token can pass every check and still lose value. Never invest money you can't afford to lose.

Before you start

You need the token's contract address (copy it from the project's official site or a trusted aggregator, never from a DM or a reply) and the right block explorer for its chain: Etherscan for Ethereum, Basescan for Base, BscScan for BNB Chain, Solscan for Solana. Free scanners such as GoPlus, Token Sniffer, De.Fi Scanner or Honeypot.is automate several of the checks below; use them, but read the contract yourself for anything that matters.

The checklist

  1. Is the contract verified? RED FLAG IF NO

    On the explorer, the "Contract" tab should show readable source code with a green check. Unverified code means nobody can see what it really does. There is rarely a good reason for a public token to hide it.

  2. What can the owner do?

    In the source, look for functions only the owner can call. The dangerous ones: mint (create new tokens at will), pause or setTradingEnabled (freeze selling), blacklist / setBot (block specific wallets from selling), and setFee / setTax with no upper limit (raise the sell tax to 99%).

  3. Is ownership renounced or behind a timelock?

    Check the owner() value on the "Read Contract" tab. The zero address means renounced. A multisig or a timelock contract is better than a single wallet. A single fresh wallet holding all the powers above is the classic rug setup.

  4. Is the contract upgradeable (a proxy)?

    If the explorer says "Proxy" or the code uses upgradeTo, whoever controls the proxy can replace the logic tomorrow. Renounced ownership means little if the code itself can be swapped.

  5. Can you actually sell? (the honeypot test) CRITICAL

    Honeypots let you buy but block or tax-to-zero your sells. Run the address through a sell simulator (Honeypot.is, GoPlus, De.Fi). Then look at the token's transfer history: if you see many buys and almost no successful sells from ordinary wallets, walk away.

  6. What are the buy and sell taxes?

    Simulators report them. Anything above about 10% deserves a reason. Watch for asymmetric taxes (2% buy, 30% sell) and taxes the owner can still change, which brings you back to check 2.

  7. How much liquidity is there, and is it locked?

    On the DEX pair, check the pool size: a pool of a few thousand dollars can be emptied by one seller. Then check who holds the LP tokens. If they are burned or locked in a known locker with a date you can verify, good. If they sit in the deployer's wallet, the deployer can pull the liquidity at any time.

  8. How concentrated are the holders?

    On the "Holders" tab, exclude the pool and known burn or lock contracts, then add up the top 10. If a handful of wallets own most of the supply, especially wallets funded by the deployer, they can crash the price whenever they choose.

  9. Who deployed it, and what did they deploy before?

    Click the creator address. A wallet that has launched many tokens, each one dead within days, is telling you its business model. Also check where its gas money came from: mixers and fresh exchange withdrawals are common in throwaway setups.

  10. Are there hidden trading limits?

    Look for maxTxAmount, maxWallet, cooldowns or "anti-bot" lists the owner can edit. These can be legitimate at launch, but if the owner can tighten them later, they can stop you from selling.

  11. Is the audit real?

    "Audited" badges are easy to fake. Find the report on the auditor's own website, check it covers this exact contract address, and read the open findings. An audit of a different address, or one with unresolved critical issues, is worth nothing.

  12. How old and how active is the project?

    A token a few hours old, with a website registered last week and social accounts full of bought followers, carries far more risk than one with months of on-chain history. Age is not proof of safety, but brand-new everything should raise the bar for every other check.

Quick verdict

Treat any failure on checks 1, 5 or 7 as a stop: unverified code, failed sell simulation, or unlocked liquidity in the deployer's hands. Two or more problems among the others deserve the same answer. Passing everything removes the obvious traps, but it does not make the token a good investment.
